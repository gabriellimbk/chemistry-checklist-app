# Reactions

1. Explain why the addition of HBr to propene yields 2-bromopropane as the major product, rather than 1-bromopropane, in terms of reaction intermediates.
Answer: The addition of HBr to propene proceeds via an electrophilic addition mechanism. The initial step involves the protonation of the alkene by HBr. When propene is protonated, two possible carbocation intermediates can form: a primary carbocation (C H two Br C H two C H two plus) or a secondary carbocation (C H three C H plus C H three). The secondary carbocation is more stable than the primary carbocation due to hyperconjugation and inductive effects from the two alkyl groups. Therefore, the formation of the secondary carbocation is kinetically favoured. The bromide ion then attacks this more stable secondary carbocation, leading to two bromopropane as the major product.

2. Describe a chemical test to distinguish between propene and propane, including expected observations.
Answer: A chemical test to distinguish between propene and propane is the Baeyer's test, which uses cold, dilute, alkaline potassium permanganate solution. Propene, being an alkene with a carbon-carbon double bond, will react with the permanganate. The purple colour of the permanganate solution will disappear, and a brown precipitate of manganese dioxide will form, as the propene is mildly oxidized to propane-one, two-diol. Propane, being an alkane, does not have a carbon-carbon double bond and is unreactive towards cold, dilute, alkaline potassium permanganate. Therefore, the purple colour of the solution will persist, and no brown precipitate will be observed.

3. Outline the mechanism for the electrophilic addition of bromine to propene.
Answer: The mechanism for the electrophilic addition of bromine to propene involves two main steps. First, the electron-rich pi bond of propene acts as a nucleophile and attacks the electrophilic bromine molecule. This causes the bromine-bromine bond to break heterolytically, forming a cyclic bromonium ion intermediate and a bromide ion. The bromonium ion is a three-membered ring containing the two carbon atoms of the original double bond and a positively charged bromine atom. In the second step, the bromide ion, acting as a nucleophile, attacks one of the carbon atoms of the bromonium ion from the opposite side to the bromine bridge. This anti-addition opens the cyclic intermediate and forms one, two dibromopropane.
